Operation Low Tide: inside a living-off-the-land intrusion at regional freight operators

A phishing email disguised as a shipping manifest was the opening move in a quiet, monthslong intrusion. Here's how the cluster we track as SILENT EGRET moved through seven logistics networks — and the detection logic that caught them.

Priya Nandakumar, Senior Threat Intelligence Analyst · Published June 10, 2026 · 6-minute read

Key findings

  • SILENT EGRET compromised at least seven freight and customs-brokerage firms across the Asia-Pacific region between March and May 2026.
  • Initial access relied on a spear-phishing email carrying a shortcut file disguised as a shipping manifest.
  • The actor relied almost entirely on built-in Windows tools, leaving little for signature-based antivirus to catch.
  • Data was staged and exfiltrated through a cloud sync client already trusted in the environment.

Overview

In March 2026, we began investigating anomalous scheduled-task activity at a freight forwarder in Singapore. The same pattern turned up at six more logistics firms over the following weeks. We track the activity as Operation Low Tide, attributed with moderate confidence to a cluster we call SILENT EGRET. Dwell time across victims ranged from three to eleven weeks — the operators favored blending in over speed.

Initial access and execution

Victims received a phishing email referencing a real-looking shipment number, with a ZIP attachment containing a shortcut file disguised as a PDF manifest. Opening it invoked a legitimate, signed Windows utility to fetch a second-stage PowerShell loader that ran entirely in memory — no standalone executable ever touched disk, which is why several victims' allow-listing policies didn't flag it.

Persistence and lateral movement

The loader persisted via a scheduled task disguised as a routine browser update, running every six hours. From there, the operators harvested locally cached credentials and moved laterally over SMB. In three environments, one of those credentials belonged to a domain administrator who had logged in for routine support — a reminder of how far a stale login can travel.

The thing that stood out wasn't a single clever exploit. It was how ordinary everything looked.— Priya Nandakumar, lead analyst on the investigation

Exfiltration

Rather than standing up dedicated infrastructure, SILENT EGRET staged compressed archives and uploaded them through a cloud sync client already installed and trusted in the environment — traffic that looked like routine business file sync.

Detection

Two signals gave the intrusion away: an unusual pattern of scheduled-task creation events (Event ID 4698) tied to a shortcut-execution chain, and outbound connections to domains registered fewer than 30 days earlier, outside business hours. A simplified version of the rule we used:

title: Suspicious scheduled task from shortcut execution
detection:
  task_created: {EventID: 4698, ParentImage|endswith: '\mshta.exe'}
  young_domain: {dest_domain_age_days|lt: 30}
  off_hours: {event_hour_local|not_between: [7, 19]}
  condition: task_created and (young_domain or off_hours)
level: high

Recommendations

  • Block or sandbox .lnk files arriving as email attachments, especially inside archives.
  • Alert on scheduled-task creation where the parent process is a scripting utility, not an installer.
  • Score outbound connections by domain age — SILENT EGRET's infrastructure was consistently under 30 days old.
  • Audit which support accounts have logged into end-user workstations, and scope their privileges accordingly.
